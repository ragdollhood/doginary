Dog Weather Safety Guide: How Every Season Affects Your Dog

The best weather for dogs isn't the same for every dog. Temperature, humidity, wind, rain, snow and even air quality all affect how safe and comfortable a walk really is. This guide covers dog safety in hot weather, dog safety in cold weather, and everything in between — based on advice from veterinary and animal welfare organisations.

Weather affects more than your walking plans — it affects your dog's health and safety. Dogs cool themselves mainly by panting and through limited sweating in their paw pads, so they experience heat, cold, wind and rain very differently than we do. Understanding these risks helps you choose the best time to walk, exercise and care for your dog throughout the year.

HOT DAYS

Hot weather and dogs

Hot weather is one of the most common weather-related dangers for dogs — and one of the most preventable.

Unlike humans, dogs mainly cool themselves through panting and limited sweating from their paw pads. This makes dog safety in hot weather a real concern, especially during exercise or in humid conditions, since dogs can overheat far faster than people realise.

Dogs at highest risk

  • Puppies
  • Senior dogs
  • Overweight dogs
  • Dogs with heart or respiratory conditions
  • Flat-faced breeds such as Bulldogs, Pugs and French Bulldogs

Signs of heat stress — contact a vet immediately

  • Excessive panting
  • Heavy drooling
  • Bright red gums
  • Weakness or unsteadiness
  • Collapse or loss of consciousness

Best practices:

  • Walk early in the morning or late in the evening.
  • Always provide access to fresh water.
  • Make sure your dog has access to shade.
  • Never leave a dog in a parked vehicle.
  • Reduce exercise during very hot weather.

Hot pavement can burn dog paws

Many dog owners watch the air temperature but forget the ground temperature. Surface temperatures on asphalt, concrete and similar materials can run 40–60°F (22–33°C) hotter than the air. On an 86°F (30°C) day, asphalt can reach roughly 135°F (57°C) — hot enough to burn a dog's paw pads within seconds.

The 7-second test

  1. Place the back of your hand on the pavement.
  2. Hold it there for 7 seconds.
  3. If it feels too hot for your hand, it's too hot for your dog's paws.
WINTER

Cold weather and dogs

Some dogs love winter, while others struggle in the cold — dog safety in cold weather comes down to coat, size and health.

Cold weather can increase the risk of hypothermia, frostbite, joint discomfort and reduced mobility in older dogs. Smaller breeds, short-haired dogs, puppies and senior dogs generally have less cold tolerance than thick-coated breeds.

Winter safety tips:

  • Shorten walks during extreme cold.
  • Dry paws thoroughly after walks.
  • Consider a coat for small or short-haired dogs.
  • Watch for ice and frozen surfaces.
  • Provide a warm resting area indoors.
WET WEATHER

Rainy weather

Rain itself is rarely dangerous, but prolonged damp conditions can still create problems.

Potential concerns include slippery surfaces, reduced visibility, chilling after walks, and mud or contaminants on paws. Dogs with arthritis or mobility issues may also become less comfortable in wet conditions — this varies between individual dogs.

After rainy walks:

  • Dry your dog thoroughly.
  • Check paws for debris.
  • Wash muddy paws if necessary.

Wind and strong gusts

Wind is often overlooked but can affect dogs significantly. Strong winds may carry debris, increase discomfort in cold weather, reduce visibility and create anxiety in some dogs. Pay special attention to small breeds, senior dogs, dogs with eye problems, and nervous dogs sensitive to environmental changes. Shorter walks may be more appropriate during periods of strong wind, particularly when wind combines with cold temperatures.

THUNDERSTORMS

Thunderstorms and lightning

Many dogs are sensitive to thunder, lightning and sudden changes in air pressure.

Common behaviours include hiding, shaking, excessive barking, pacing and refusing to go outside. Provide a safe, calm indoor environment and avoid outdoor activities during thunderstorms whenever possible. For severe fear, a vet can advise on sound training, calming aids or prescription medication.

Snow and ice

Snow can be fun, but it introduces its own hazards: ice-covered sidewalks, salted roads, frostbite risk, and snow buildup between paw pads. Salt and chemical de-icers can also irritate paws and skin.

After snowy walks:

  • Inspect paw pads.
  • Remove packed snow.
  • Rinse off de-icing chemicals.
  • Dry paws thoroughly.

Air quality matters too

Weather isn't just temperature. Poor air quality can affect dogs with respiratory disease, senior dogs, flat-faced breeds and dogs with heart conditions. When air quality is poor, shorten outdoor exercise, avoid strenuous activity, and monitor for coughing or breathing difficulties.

When weather requires extra caution

Certain dogs need extra protection in almost every season. Be especially careful with:

  • Puppies
  • Senior dogs
  • Overweight dogs
  • Brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds
  • Dogs with heart disease
  • Dogs with respiratory disease

Final thoughts

There is no single "best weather for dogs" that fits every dog. The safest approach is to consider temperature, humidity, pavement temperature, wind, precipitation, and your individual dog's age, breed and health status. Small adjustments to walking times and outdoor activities can significantly reduce weather-related risks and help your dog stay healthy all year long.
